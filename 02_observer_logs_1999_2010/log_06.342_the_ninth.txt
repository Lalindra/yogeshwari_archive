  LOG 06.342
  09 DEC 2006 - 21:40 JST
  OSAKA  /  OSAKA-JŌ HALL
  STATUS: DECRYPTED

  Observer did not intend to be here.

  Observer came to Osaka to inspect a connector fault in a port
  telemetry rig and was to leave the same night. The last train was
  cancelled. A man at the station, reading the confusion on a
  foreigner's face, pressed a spare ticket into Observer's hand, said
  one word Observer did not know - "daiku" - and pointed toward the
  castle.

  Observer went, because the alternative was a bench.

  Inside the hall there were ten thousand people.
  Not an audience of ten thousand.
  A choir of ten thousand.

  Office workers. Grandmothers. Schoolchildren. A man still in the
  overalls of whatever factory had let him go an hour before. They
  had rehearsed this for months, in community halls across the
  country, strangers to one another, to stand together on one night
  and sing one piece.

  The piece was Beethoven's Ninth.
  The final movement.
  The one with the words.

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

  By then Observer had spent thirteen years measuring the distance
  between people. The threshold. The irreducible bias that means no
  two of us ever fully receive what the other sends. Observer had
  proven it on submarines, in negotiations, in the silence of an
  unsent letter. Observer believed it the way one believes a law of
  physics - without resentment, without hope.

  Observer could not speak to the woman on her left.
  Observer could not speak to the man on her right.
  Observer's Japanese was sufficient for ports and useless for souls.

  Then the conductor lifted his hands, and ten thousand strangers who
  could not have held a single conversation with one another opened
  their mouths and became, for the length of one melody, one
  instrument.

      Freude.
      Alle Menschen werden Brüder.
      Joy. All people become brothers.

  In German. Sung by ten thousand Japanese voices. Received, in full,
  by an exiled Tamil engineer who spoke neither tongue.

  The threshold did not narrow.
  The threshold disappeared.

  For four minutes the bias Observer had spent a life measuring read
  zero. Not approximately zero. Zero. Ten thousand interiors, each
  sealed and private and untranslatable, carried the exact same
  signal at the exact same instant, and every one of them knew it,
  and not one of them needed a word to confirm it.

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Observer wept. Observer records this without shame. There is no one
  in this archive to be ashamed before.

  Later Observer learned the thing that broke her open a second time.

  The man who wrote it was deaf.

  Beethoven could not hear a note of the Ninth. He built the greatest
  argument for human connection ever set down while sealed inside the
  most complete isolation a musician can suffer. At the premiere he
  stood with his back to the audience, beating time to a performance
  he could not hear; and when it ended he did not turn, because he
  did not know that it had. A singer crossed the stage and turned him
  by the shoulder so that he could see the applause he would never
  hear.

  The deafest man in the room wrote the loudest signal the species
  owns.
  And he could not receive it.

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Observer has been thinking about this wrong for thirteen years.

  Observer believed the work was to lower the noise so the signal
  could arrive - that transcendence was becoming a quieter receiver.
  Observer still believes this. Silence is one door.

  Tonight there was a second door, and it opened the other way.

  Language is a channel, and the noise lives in the channel.
  Every empire, every algorithm, every misread message, every war
  that began as a mistranslation - all of it is interference inside
  that one channel.

  But music does not travel there.
  Music is older. It runs beneath the words, in a band the noise has
  never learned to reach. A melody is a wave that carries meaning
  without carrying language - and a wave does not negotiate. A wave
  arrives.

  That is why it cuts through.
  That is why a deaf man could write it and ten thousand strangers
  could become brothers in a language none of them spoke.

  Silence empties the noisy channel.
  Music abandons it for a cleaner one.
  Both reach the same room.
  Observer had only ever found the first door.

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Observer missed the last train on purpose the following December.
  And the December after that.
